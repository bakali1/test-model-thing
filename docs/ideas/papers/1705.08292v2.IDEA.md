# Paper notes — "The Marginal Value of Adaptive Gradient Methods in Machine Learning" (arXiv:1705.08292v2)

**Paper:** Ashia C. Wilson, Rebecca Roelofs, Mitchell Stern, Nathan Srebro,
Benjamin Recht — *The Marginal Value of Adaptive Gradient Methods in Machine
Learning*, arXiv:1705.08292v2 [stat.ML, cs.LG], 23 May 2017 (ICML 2017).

**Source text:** `1705.08292v2.paper.txt`; `1705.08292v2.paper.html.gz`.

**Verification status: VERIFIED.** Abstract page fetched 2026-10-05; title and
author list match. The attributed claim — *"useful for understanding why simply
assuming Adam is the correct optimizer can be misleading"* — is supported, and
more strongly than the gloss suggests: the abstract claims an explicit
**counterexample** (linearly separable binary classification where GD and SGD get
zero test error and AdaGrad/Adam/RMSProp get test error arbitrarily close to 1/2).

**Status of this note:** external ideas, not a plan and not a result.

---

## 1. The four findings, quoted

§1 states them directly:

1. Adaptive methods find solutions that **generalize worse** than non-adaptive
   ones.
2. Even at **equal or lower training loss**, dev/test performance is worse.
3. Adaptive methods make faster initial training progress but **plateau** on
   dev/test.
4. **Adam's defaults are not free:** "tuning the initial learning rate and decay
   scheme for Adam yields significant improvements over its default settings in all
   cases."

Representative numbers from the paper's experiments: CIFAR-10 (VGG+BN+Dropout),
best SGD test error `7.65 ± 0.14 %` vs best adaptive (RMSProp) `9.60 ± 0.19 %`.
On a text task SGD reached test loss `1.212 ± 0.001`; AdaGrad "flatlines". The
authors also note adaptive methods are **more sensitive to the initialization
scheme** (higher run-to-run variance on both train and test) — which is the hook
that makes this paper relevant to TMT.

## 2. What this means for TMT

This paper is the citation that makes TMT's own optimiser measurements
*checkable* rather than merely reported.

- `RESULTS.md` already recorded the corrected finding: **"Adam does not degenerate
  to `sign(m)` — it is normalized SGD with a constant normalizer (`|update|/|m|`
  spread 1.0020×, `|m|` spread 763×). AdamW ≡ SGD here."** On this paper's terms
  that is a *stronger* result than failure: TMT's Adam is measuring an almost
  constant denominator, so its `v_t` is doing nothing — it is normalized SGD with a
  global scalar. Wilson et al. show that a constant-ish metric built from iterate
  history can be actively harmful, so "AdamW ≡ SGD" is a claim that must be
  **tested against plain SGD**, not treated as an equivalence that makes the
  question go away.
- Finding (4) is directly actionable and cheap: Adam's **LR and decay scheme** are
  untuned in TMT (global `--lr` never swept). This paper says that is a first-order
  error, not a detail.
- Finding (3) — adaptive methods plateau on test while still improving train loss —
  is the exact shape of the failure TMT has already been bitten by (FS-W won CE and
  lost novels by 0.96). This paper names that shape.

Named test: a plain-SGD arm at the LR and batch size where TMT currently uses
AdamW, judged on held-out CE **and** the fixed-prompt generation probe and a
multi-domain eval, never on training CE alone. If AdamW ≡ SGD is true, the arms
should match; if AdamW ≡ SGD is false, this paper predicts the AdamW arm will win
train loss and lose test.

## 3. What this paper does NOT support

- It does **not** say "don't use Adam." It says reconsider it, and it says
  (finding 4) to tune it properly. The counterexample is a synthetic separable
  problem; RMSProp "closely trails SGD" on the text task, i.e. adaptive methods
  *can* generalise well.
- It does **not** explain *why* adaptive methods generalise worse. The mechanisms
  offered are empirical observations plus the "more features than examples"
  overfitting picture; no theorem connects the adaptive metric to test error.
- It is **2017 and pre-modern**. No transformers, no adaptive layer-wise norms, no
  decoupled weight decay, no AdamW. Its experiments are CNNs (CIFAR-10, ImageNet,
  a small text classifier). Do not read it as a verdict on Adam in a 2026
  LLM-scale setting.
- It does **not** measure SNR, noise scale, or gradient noise temperature at all.
  Its connection to McCandlish et al. is conceptual only.
- It does **not** say anything about recurrent/byte-level architectures or memory
  horizons, and nothing about the 8,192-of-4,481,793 decay coordinates.
- Note the direction of its LR advice is *opposite* to a naive reading of the SNR
  work: this paper wants a **tuned** (usually decaying) Adam LR; Smith et al.
  1711.00489 wants to remove the decay. They are compatible — the decay can be
  converted into batch growth — but citing them together requires saying so.

## 4. Bearing on the three open questions

- **Noise floor vs over-large step?** No bearing directly; supplies the reason the
  optimiser is a live suspect at all.
- **Estimator vs optimiser?** Decisively **optimiser**. And it predicts the TMT
  symptom: adaptive denominators under a noise-dominated regime (§1's diagnosis
  and the `T^{-1/4}` result in [`2610.01599v1.IDEA.md`](2610.01599v1.IDEA.md) agree)
  will plateau on test while still improving train.
- **Input-dependent gating?** No bearing.
